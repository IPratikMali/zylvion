# PR and News Rewrite Guidelines
## For Journalist Teams Writing Original POV Pieces from Source Coverage

> **Purpose:** A complete system for journalists reading original news sources and writing 400 to 500 word pieces in the publication's voice, with analysis or reframed POV, proper source attribution, and SEO-ready structure.

---

## The Core Principle

This is not syndication. It is not paraphrasing. It is not summarizing.

A PR news piece takes a published story as its starting point and produces a genuinely different piece of journalism. The original source supplies the facts. Your publication supplies the angle, the analysis, the context, and the voice.

The reader who has already read the original source should find something new in your piece. The reader who has not read it should get everything they need from yours.

If your piece could be described as "the same article with different words," it needs to be rewritten from scratch.

---

## Part 1: Before You Write

### 1.1 Source Evaluation

Not every published story deserves coverage. Before starting a piece, the journalist answers four questions:

- Is the original story from a credible source?
  Press releases, wire reports, established publications, and primary documents all qualify.
- Is this story relevant to this publication's topic area and audience?
- Does this publication have a distinct angle or POV to add? If not, do not cover it.
- Is the story timely?
  Pieces published more than 72 hours after the original lose most value unless significant new analysis is added.

If any of these answers is no, pass on the story or flag it for a different section of the publication.

### 1.2 Read the Original Completely Before Writing Anything

Read the full original source before forming an angle. Do not skim for facts to reuse. Read it as a journalist would: understanding what the story is, what it does not say, what questions it raises, and where it could be pushed further.

After reading, write down in one sentence:
- What the original story says
- What your publication's angle adds that the original does not cover

That second sentence is your article.

### 1.3 Identify Your Angle Before Writing

Every PR news piece needs a clear angle. The angle is the reason this publication is covering this story, and the reason a reader who has already seen the headline elsewhere would read yours.

**Angle types:**

**Industry implication:** The story is about a product launch, a study, a policy change, or a business event. Your angle is what it means for a specific industry or audience segment.

Example: Google announces a new Chrome feature. Original coverage reports the feature. Your angle: what this means for enterprise IT teams managing Chrome at scale.

**The overlooked detail:** The original story focuses on one aspect. Your publication digs into a detail the original glossed over.

Example: A funding round announcement. The original covers the valuation. Your angle: the lead investor's track record in this sector and what their involvement signals about where the market is heading.

**The practical takeaway:** The original is informational. Your piece converts it to actionable.

Example: A cybersecurity report. The original covers the findings. Your angle: the three specific things businesses should do this week in response.

**The broader context:** The story is one data point in a larger trend. Your publication connects the dots.

Example: A retailer closes 200 locations. The original covers the announcement. Your angle: this as part of a broader pattern in physical retail that three other events this quarter also signal.

**The skeptical read:** The story presents something as straightforwardly positive or straightforwardly negative. Your publication applies critical analysis.

Example: A company announces a major sustainability initiative. Your angle: whether the numbers behind the initiative actually support the headline claim.

## Part 2: Voice and Tone

### 2.1 The Two Voice Modes

PR news pieces use one of two voice modes depending on the topic and the publication's identity for that piece.

**Third person news voice:**
Objective framing. The publication reports and analyzes. No "we" or "I." Used when the piece is primarily factual reporting with analytical framing. Appropriate for hard news, business news, policy, and financial coverage.

"Google's latest update to Chrome's password manager introduces a feature that enterprise IT administrators have requested since 2021. Whether it solves the underlying problem depends on how organizations have structured their credential policies."

**First person plural editorial voice:**
The publication takes a position. "We think," "our read," "what this tells us." Used when the piece is explicitly analytical or opinion-driven. Appropriate when the publication is known for commentary, not just reporting.

"Our read on this acquisition is straightforward: the price paid makes sense only if the acquirer has a product roadmap the market has not seen yet. The announced synergies do not justify the multiple on their own."

**The rule on mixing:**
A piece can open in third person and shift to first person plural for the analysis section. It cannot shift back and forth within the same paragraph. Decide the mode for each section before writing it.

### 2.2 What "POV" Means in Practice

POV does not mean opinion for its own sake. It means the publication has a specific perspective, informed by expertise, that shapes how it reads and presents the news.

POV shows up as:
- Naming what the story does not say that the reader should know
- Connecting the story to a trend the publication has been tracking
- Applying a specific lens (industry expertise, regional context, audience-specific implications) that general coverage does not
- Giving the reader a verdict: what this means, what to watch for, what to do

POV does not show up as:
- Personal opinion without factual grounding
- Speculation presented as analysis
- Negativity without evidence
- Advocacy for a position that the facts do not support

### 2.3 Tone Calibration by Topic

| Topic Type | Tone | What to Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Hard business news | Measured, precise, no hyperbole | Excitement, alarm without evidence |
| Technology launches | Analytical, skeptical where warranted | Pure enthusiasm or pure cynicism |
| Policy and regulation | Neutral framing, clear implications | Political positioning |
| Industry trends | Contextual, pattern-aware | Overconfident predictions |
| Research and studies | Evidence-first, caveat where needed | Overstating what the study actually shows |
| Company announcements | Balanced, read between the lines | Taking the press release at face value |

## Part 3: Structure and Format

### 3.1 The 400 to 500 Word Structure

Every PR news piece follows this structure. It is not optional. The word count is strict. Going significantly over means the piece has not been edited. Going significantly under means the angle was not fully developed.

```
Headline
Subheadline (optional, 1 line)
Body: 400 to 500 words
Source attribution block
```

### 3.2 The Headline

The headline is not the same as the original story's headline. It reflects your publication's angle, not the original's framing.

**Headline rules:**
- States the angle, not just the topic
- Under 70 characters for SEO
- No clickbait that the piece does not back up
- No em dashes
- Sentence case
- Contains the primary keyword naturally

**Original headline:** "Google Rolls Out New Password Manager Feature for Chrome"
**Your headline (industry implication angle):** "Chrome's New Password Manager Feature Has One Problem Enterprise IT Will Spot Immediately"
**Your headline (practical takeaway angle):** "What Google's Chrome Password Update Means for Your Business Security Setup"

### 3.3 The Opening (50 to 80 words)

The opening is not a summary of the original story. It is the entry point for your angle.

Open with the most interesting or provocative thing your publication has to say about this story. The fact that it happened is not interesting. What it means, what it signals, or what it contradicts. That is interesting.

Do not open with: "[Company] announced today that..."
Do not open with: "A new report has found that..."
Do not open with: "According to [original source]..."

These openings exist in every news piece on the internet. They tell the reader nothing they could not get from a headline scan.

**Open with your angle instead:**

"The feature enterprise Chrome administrators have been requesting for three years is finally here. Whether it solves the problem they actually have is a different question."

That opening tells the reader: you know this space, you have a take, and the take is more nuanced than the press release.

### 3.4 The Body (280 to 360 words)

The body carries the facts, the analysis, and the context. It is organized by what the reader needs to know in what order, not by what the original story covered in what order.

**Paragraph rules (same as article framework):**
- Maximum 3 lines per paragraph
- No paragraph exists just to repeat what the previous one said
- Each paragraph advances the piece by one idea

**Fact integration:**
Facts from the original source are woven into your analysis, not listed first and analyzed second. The reader should not be able to identify where the original source ends and your analysis begins. They should feel like one integrated piece of journalism.

**What to include:**
- The core facts the reader needs to understand the story
- The context that makes those facts meaningful
- Your publication's specific analytical contribution
- One forward-looking sentence: what to watch for, what happens next, or what question this raises

**What to cut:**
- Any fact from the original that does not serve your angle
- Background information the reader can find anywhere
- Filler that exists to reach word count
- Any sentence that the original source already said better

### 3.5 The Closing (60 to 80 words)

The closing lands the piece. It is not a summary. It is the final analytical point or the forward-looking verdict.

**Closing patterns that work:**

**The open question:** End with the question this story raises that nobody has answered yet. Makes the reader feel the conversation is ongoing.

"The real question is whether [company] has the distribution to make this matter at scale. The product is ready. The market access is not obvious yet."

**The verdict:** A direct statement of what your publication thinks this means.

"This is not the disruption the headlines suggest. It is an incremental move by a company that needed a headline. The market will price it accordingly within a quarter."

**The pattern close:** Connect this story to the broader trend it is part of.

"This is the third announcement in this category in six weeks. When three separate companies move in the same direction in the same window, that is not coincidence. That is a market signal."

### 3.6 The Source Attribution Block

Every PR news piece ends with a source attribution block. This is mandatory. It is placed after the body and before any tags or metadata.

**Format:**

> This piece was inspired by coverage at [Publication Name]. Check out their take on [Original Article Title as hyperlink] for the original story.

**Rules for the attribution block:**
- "Inspired by" is the correct framing. Not "sourced from." Not "based on." Not "copied from."
- Link the original article title as anchor text.
  Never use "click here" or "original story." The actual title is the anchor.
- Name the publication naturally in the sentence before the link.
- One attribution block per piece.
  Multiple sources: list all with individual links in the same block.
- The attribution block does not count toward the 400 to 500 word count.

**Example:**

> This piece was inspired by coverage at WebTechnoto. Check out their take on [40 Google Chrome Shortcuts For MacBook](https://webtechnoto.com/blog/40-google-chrome-shortcuts-for-macbook) for the original story.

## Part 4: SEO Considerations for PR News Pieces

### 4.1 Keyword Targeting

PR news pieces target keywords differently from long-form articles. The timeframe is shorter and the intent is more specific.

Target one of these keyword types per piece:

**News query keywords:** Searches that spike when a story breaks. "[Company name] [announcement topic]", "[product name] [feature]". These have high short-term volume and low competition because the story is fresh.

**Evergreen angle keywords:** The analytical framing of the story may target a longer-lived keyword. "What [event] means for [industry]" often performs beyond the news cycle if the analysis stays relevant.

**Do not target:** Keywords with no connection to the story's news hook. A PR piece should not be keyword-padded with tangential terms just to chase volume.

### 4.2 Meta Title and Description for PR Pieces

**Meta title formula for news pieces:**
`[Your angle on the story]: [What happened in 4 to 6 words]`

Example: `Chrome's Password Manager Update: What IT Teams Need to Know`

**Meta description formula:**
One sentence on what happened. One sentence on your publication's take. Under 155 characters total.

Example: `Google updated Chrome's password manager with enterprise features. Here is what it actually solves, and what it still does not.`

### 4.3 Duplicate Content Risk

A PR news piece that is too close to the original source risks being treated as duplicate content by Google.

The protection is your angle. If your piece:
- Opens differently from the original
- Covers different aspects or leads with different emphasis
- Adds analysis the original does not contain
- Has a different headline and meta description

Google treats it as a distinct piece. The source attribution block reinforces that your piece acknowledges the original and is not attempting to pass it off as independent reporting.

If you are unsure whether a piece is distinct enough, run the opening paragraph of both pieces side by side. If they are covering the same ground in the same order, your piece needs a different opening and a stronger angle.

## Part 5: What Good Looks Like vs. What Fails

### 5.1 Good PR Piece Signals

- The headline does not appear on any other site covering the same story
- The opening would make sense even if the reader has not seen the original source
- The piece contains at least one analytical insight not present in the original
- A reader who finishes your piece learns something they would not get from the original headline alone
- The source attribution is present, correctly formatted, and links to the original

### 5.2 Failure Modes

**The paraphrase failure:**
The piece covers the same points in the same order as the original, with different words. This is the most common failure. The fix is identifying your angle before writing and building the piece around that angle, not around the original's structure.

**The summary failure:**
The piece is a condensed version of the original with no analysis added. A 400-word summary of a 1,200-word article is not journalism. It is an abstract. Add your publication's perspective or do not cover the story.

**The opinion without grounding failure:**
The piece takes a strong position that the facts do not support. POV requires evidence. An opinion unsupported by the facts in the original story or by publicly verifiable context is not analysis. It is speculation. Speculation published as journalism damages the publication's credibility.

**The missing attribution failure:**
The piece runs without the source attribution block. This is a non-negotiable requirement. Every piece that draws on external coverage must attribute it.

**The over-long failure:**
The piece exceeds 500 words because the journalist included everything from the original source plus analysis. The edit should cut facts that do not serve the angle, not the analysis. The analysis is the reason the piece exists.

## Part 6: The Journalist's Pre-Publish Checklist

**Angle and originality**
- [ ] The angle is identified and written in one sentence before writing began
- [ ] The opening does not start with "[Company] announced" or "According to"
- [ ] The piece contains at least one analytical point not in the original source
- [ ] The headline is different from the original story's headline and reflects your angle

**Structure and format**
- [ ] Total word count is between 400 and 500 words (attribution block excluded)
- [ ] No paragraph exceeds 3 lines
- [ ] The closing is a verdict, an open question, or a pattern close, not a summary
- [ ] No em dashes anywhere in the piece

**Voice and tone**
- [ ] Voice mode is decided (third person, first person plural, or mixed with clear section breaks)
- [ ] No speculation presented as fact
- [ ] No opinion without factual grounding in the piece or from verifiable context

**SEO**
- [ ] Headline is under 70 characters and reflects the angle keyword
- [ ] Meta description is under 155 characters
- [ ] Opening paragraph does not replicate the original source's opening

**Attribution**
- [ ] Source attribution block is present at the end of the piece
- [ ] Original article title is used as the anchor text in the attribution link
- [ ] Attribution block is correctly formatted per the standard

## Part 7: Quick Reference: Attribution Block Formats

**Single source:**
> This piece was inspired by coverage at [Publication Name]. Check out their take on [Original Article Title](URL) for the original story.

**Two sources:**
> This piece draws on reporting from [Publication A] and [Publication B]. Original coverage: [Article Title A](URL) and [Article Title B](URL).

**Primary source plus supporting data:**
> This piece was inspired by coverage at [Publication Name]. The underlying data comes from [Research Institution or Report Name](URL). Check out [Original Article Title](URL) for the original story.
