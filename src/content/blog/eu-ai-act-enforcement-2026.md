The EU AI Act reached its first major enforcement milestone on February 2, 2026, with prohibitions on unacceptable-risk AI systems now enforceable. The August 2, 2026 deadline for high-risk AI system obligations is approaching — and the compliance picture is murkier than the headlines suggest.

## What's Actually Required for High-Risk Systems

High-risk AI systems — covering areas like biometric identification, critical infrastructure management, employment screening, credit scoring, and AI in education — must comply with:

1. **Risk management systems**: Documented, ongoing risk assessment processes integrated into the development lifecycle
2. **Data governance**: Training, validation, and test data must be subject to data governance practices examining biases, gaps, and shortcomings
3. **Technical documentation**: A complete technical file demonstrating conformity, kept up to date
4. **Transparency and logging**: Automatic event logging with EU-specified metadata fields
5. **Human oversight**: Technical measures enabling effective human oversight and intervention
6. **Accuracy, robustness, cybersecurity**: Performance metrics must meet defined thresholds with evidence

The conformity assessment process — the path to the CE marking required before market placement — can be self-assessed for most high-risk AI systems, but AI systems in biometrics and critical infrastructure require third-party assessment.

## The Fine Structure

| Violation Category | Maximum Fine |
|-------------------|-------------|
| Prohibited AI practices | €35M or 7% global annual turnover |
| High-risk system violations | €15M or 3% global annual turnover |
| Providing incorrect information to authorities | €7.5M or 1.5% global annual turnover |

For context, a company with €10B annual revenue could face a maximum fine of €700M for a prohibited practice violation. The FTC maximum in the US for a comparable violation would be significantly lower.

## Who Is Actually Exposed

The practical enforcement risk is concentrated on:

- **General purpose AI model providers** with 10B+ parameter models (specific obligations kicked in February 2026)
- **Recruitment AI vendors** operating in EU member states
- **Credit and insurance scoring AI** used for EU consumers
- **Biometric surveillance** companies (most prohibited outright)

US tech companies with EU business are subject to the Act if their AI systems affect people in the EU, regardless of where the company is headquartered. This extraterritorial application is similar to GDPR.

## First Enforcement Actions

The EU AI Office — the newly established regulator — has opened preliminary investigations into three GPAI model providers as of publication. No fines have been issued yet, but requests for technical documentation have been sent to multiple US AI labs.

Member state regulators have more discretion than under GDPR: each country can designate its own national competent authority, and enforcement consistency will likely be uneven in the early years.

## What Companies Should Actually Do

If you're a tech company with EU exposure and haven't started AI Act compliance work, you're already behind. The minimum viable compliance posture:

1. Inventory all AI systems touching EU users and classify them under the Act's risk categories
2. For high-risk systems, begin the conformity assessment process now — it takes months
3. Establish a documentation and logging infrastructure before the August deadline
4. Designate an EU-based authorized representative if you're a non-EU provider

The Act is more enforceable than skeptics predicted. The EU AI Office has budget, staff, and political support. Expect the first significant fines within 18 months.
